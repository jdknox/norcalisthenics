# How to get flexible one time, forever
2025-08-08

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NwX2dh0dwNA) Woods admits "I don't really put much time or thought into flexibility" — because of how he built it initially.

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript. This is the fuller theoretical explanation of his end-range-strength flexibility method (companion to the guided lower-body routine covered in the separately summarized "5 minute session" video), demonstrated here specifically on the pike (toe-touch) movement.

## Core mechanism (fullest stated version of the nervous-system model used throughout his mobility content)
- Uses a scenario to illustrate the underlying logic: landing in an extreme, uncontrolled range of motion (e.g. an accidental splits position under load) with insufficient strength to control or exit it risks serious joint damage — so the nervous system pre-emptively limits range of motion via involuntary muscle contraction, before a joint's true structural limit is reached, specifically to prevent landing in positions the person isn't strong enough to safely control.
- States this restriction is involuntary and automatic — not a conscious choice, and not caused by physically "short" muscles (explicitly rejects the "muscles are like rubber bands" framing).
- The fix: build strength specifically in the range near the current limit, which demonstrates to the nervous system that the position is controllable/safe, permanently and gradually extending the range it permits — described as functionally identical to how strength training raises a person's safe working capacity, just applied to range of motion rather than load.
- States this is why properly built flexibility is durable: once built, it degrades slowly even with years of neglect, and is quick to restore if lost — mirroring muscle memory in strength training.

## Relationship to general mobility vs. specific flexibility goals
- States that ordinary full-range-of-motion strength training in the six basic movement patterns already covers essentially all functional/daily mobility needs on its own — no separate general mobility work required if that training is done properly.
- Specific flexibility goals beyond that baseline (e.g. splits, straddle positions) are treated as optional add-ons using this same method, pursued only if there's a specific movement goal that requires them.

## Demonstrated protocol — the pike
- Standing with feet together, knees locked, hinge at the hips (cue: fold the belly button toward the thighs, "closing like a book" at the hip) until involuntary tension is felt in the calves/hamstrings — that tension point is where training begins, not full end range.
- At that point, actively contract the tensed muscles as hard as possible (described as equivalent to a max-intensity strength rep) rather than trying to relax through or push past the sensation.
- Preferred execution: rather than using external weight (the classic version uses a loaded Jefferson curl with a barbell or weight vest), pull the torso deeper using the person's own upper body (hooking hands around the ankles, using hip flexors/core to pull in) while simultaneously trying to pull back out with the stretched posterior-chain muscles — creating two opposing forces.
- Performed as dynamic reps (moving in and out of the position), not static holds; as a session progresses and depth increases, deliberately lean further in to keep triggering the same involuntary-tension response and continue building strength at the new, deeper range.

## Dosage
- Roughly 10 reps as a practical benchmark, or continue until reaching diminishing returns (session done in one set).
- Frequency: once or twice a week.
- Suggests testing flexibility before and after a single session (e.g. a standing toe-touch measurement) as a way to directly observe the immediate, within-session range increase this method produces — offered as a demonstration of the mechanism's effectiveness, not evidence that one session is sufficient for lasting change.
