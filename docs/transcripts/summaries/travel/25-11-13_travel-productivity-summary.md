# Travel taught me the truth about productivity
2025-11-13

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=S2SU9G9uRf4) Woods recounts the experiment: "I recently went travelling for two months, reduced my work hours dramatically" — and became more productive, not less.

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript. This is a productivity/mindset video, not a fitness protocol, but explicitly draws a direct analogy to his training philosophy throughout.

## Core observation
- During two months of travel with dramatically reduced work hours (spent roughly equally with family, friends, and solo, while still running his business), reported his highest-ever productivity and happiness — attributed to the removal of routine/structure surfacing which of his assumed requirements for productivity were actually unnecessary.
- On returning home to unstructured free time, previous patterns of burnout/procrastination/resistance returned almost immediately — prompting him to deliberately try to recreate the travel conditions at home rather than just returning to his prior routine.

## Explicit analogy to strength training
- Applies the same rest/recovery framing used for muscle growth to cognitive work: more intense (focused) effort requires proportionally more rest, and trying to spread effort across more total hours (rather than concentrating it) doesn't yield proportionally more output — it just dilutes intensity and adds accumulated fatigue/resistance, mirroring his critique of excess training volume.

## Four changes implemented after the trip
1. **Three daily tasks, then stop.** Replaced a fixed workday-hours plan with a target of three specific, important ("needle-moving") tasks per day; once those (plus essential work) are done, the workday ends — regardless of clock time. Reports 4–6 hours of intense focus is his practical ceiling, after which output quality drops; stopping at that point (rather than continuing through fatigue) produced more total output than his previous longer, lower-intensity workdays, and removed the anxiety/guilt of an open-ended "should still be working" state.
2. **Deliberately unscheduled space in the calendar.** Argues that unstructured time (not filled with tasks) is what allows creative/cognitive work to actually happen — cites an analogy from Chris Bailey's "The Productivity Project" comparing this to traffic flow being governed by the space between cars, not the number of cars. Frames a fully-packed calendar as reducing, not increasing, effective output.
3. **A morning walk with no agenda.** A specific daily practice (walking, getting coffee, no set direction) used deliberately to reinforce a felt sense of "space" and lack of urgency before the workday starts — described as one of the most impactful small changes made.
4. **Intentional, guilt-free non-work time.** Notes that during travel, downtime (socializing, walking, eating, sightseeing) was automatically built in and never felt like "wasted" time — replicating this at home meant deliberately scheduling engaging non-work activities (increased reading being his main addition, alongside existing hobbies: tennis, golf, surfing, Spanish practice) so that free time has a clear, satisfying use rather than being filled by default with more work.

## Stated outcome
- Reports these four changes collectively reduced work hours while increasing both output and reported wellbeing/reduced stress, mirroring (and explicitly modeled on) the same "less input, more effective output" pattern he describes in his fitness content.
