# The truth I wish I knew 8 years ago
2025-10-30

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v9dbj0MM_HQ) Woods opens by admitting: "Advice is tough to give, and the older I get" the less inclined he is to give it.

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript. This is a reflective/life-advice video (three lessons for his 20-year-old self) rather than a specific physical protocol, but repeatedly draws direct parallels to his training philosophy.

## Advice #1 — Advice itself doesn't work; only doing does
- Draws on a recurring exchange with his own business coach, whose consistent answer to "how do I make good content?" was to make the process sustainable and keep doing it long-term — not a specific tactical answer.
- Extends this to fitness explicitly: a deep theoretical understanding of training science won't itself change anyone's body — only consistent action (the training and eating actually done) produces the result. Frames all advice, including his own, as inherently limited: any piece of advice can be contradicted by an equally common opposite piece of advice, so usefulness depends entirely on the listener's current context/experience, which they can't yet judge in advance.

## Advice #2 — Failure is the mechanism, not the obstacle
- Directly maps the concept of training to failure (his standard strength methodology, described throughout his other content — working at/near current maximum until output visibly drops) onto skill-learning generally: without attempting things past current ability and failing, no adaptation/improvement occurs, in fitness, language learning, content creation, or business.
- Personal example: describes his own current approach to Spanish immersion as deliberately diving into content/conversations beyond his current comprehension level, on the reasoning that discomfort/failure is what drives the fastest learning — same principle as pushing to genuine strength failure in training.
- States that the discomfort of failure is functionally similar to physical pain/discomfort and is what makes it an effective learning signal — removing that discomfort (by avoiding challenge) would remove the learning benefit too.

## Advice #3 — Simplicity and staying the course
- Argues that narrowing focus to a small, sustainable set of inputs (e.g., his own "post one video a week" rule for content, or "just read a book" as his sole initial Spanish goal) creates the space needed to actually execute consistently — paralleling his fitness system's emphasis on doing fewer things well rather than optimizing broadly.
- Warns against comparing progress to others or adding more inputs/tactics mid-process, framing this as the main way people abandon an otherwise-working long-term path.
- States that any serious pursuit "is all failure until it's not" — progress can feel invisible for a long stretch before a visible step-change, and the goal should be a sustainable process the person is willing to keep doing indefinitely (not a finish line after which the underlying behaviors stop), since he frames maintaining any result as requiring the same ongoing process that built it.
