# The truth about body image no one tells you
2025-07-17

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3WxZiiBcvpk) Woods argues "the more you focus on your body image, the harder it is" to actually make progress.

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript. This is a training-psychology video about using objective metrics instead of appearance as the measure of fitness progress — general content, not directed at any individual's personal body image.

## Three stated problems with using appearance as the measure of success
1. **Short-sighted training** — chasing visible short-term changes (a pump, feeling like a workout "did something") has little relationship to the training that actually produces meaningful long-term change, leading to inconsistent, reactive training that isn't tied to any real progression metric.
2. **Destructive dieting cycles** — if the goal is "look leaner now," there's constant pressure to keep cutting even after it stops being easy or productive, which (per his broader nutrition content) risks stalling progress, losing muscle, or triggering a restrict/rebound cycle rather than sustainable improvement.
3. **An inherently unsatisfiable standard** — since appearance can always theoretically be improved, using it as the success metric means there's never a point of genuine satisfaction; he draws a parallel to chasing happiness directly (which tends to backfire) versus pursuing it as a byproduct of concrete life domains.

## Proposed alternative — two objective proxies
### 1. Strength, as the proxy for muscle gain
- Argues visual/mirror assessment can't reliably detect ongoing muscle growth (too slow, too subjective), and that consumer body-composition tools (scales, etc.) aren't practical for frequent tracking.
- Position: for a natural, bodyweight-strength-focused approach, strength increases are a reliable proxy for muscle gain, because reaching advanced bodyweight strength standards (his examples: planche pushup, handstand pushup, one-arm chin-up, heavy single-leg squat) is not achievable without the underlying muscle tissue to produce that force — so tracking strength against defined goals removes the need to judge progress by eye at all.

### 2. Body weight trend, as the proxy for fat loss — done in short, bounded phases
- Reiterates the point made in his other nutrition content: attempting continuous fat loss becomes unproductive once it starts to feel genuinely difficult (hunger, low energy) — at that point, further cutting risks stalling or reversing muscle gain rather than producing more fat loss.
- Recommends tracking body weight (not appearance) to confirm a target rate of loss (his stated example: roughly 0.5 kg/week) is happening, and stopping the deficit once maintaining that rate becomes difficult — followed by a return to muscle-building at maintenance/surplus, which raises the muscle "floor" and makes the next leaner phase both more achievable and more comfortable.
- Frames these fat-loss and muscle-building phases as cyclical and complementary rather than a single continuous pursuit of "leaner" — his stated rough annual ratio (roughly 1 part fat-loss to 3 parts building/maintenance, up to ~12 weeks of deficit per year) matches the structure described in his separately summarized "lean vs. gains" video.

## Personal example
- Describes an early-career period of prioritizing looking leaner in the short term, hitting a wall where further fat loss stopped working, and eventually resolving this by deliberately gaining significant weight/muscle first (accepting looking less lean for a period) — reporting that the resulting higher muscle mass ultimately allowed him to reach a leaner body composition than before, at a higher total body weight, more easily than continuing to push for leanness directly.

## Overall framework
- Recommends minimizing mirror-checking specifically, and instead tracking only strength progress against defined goals and body weight trend during deficit phases — with the stated expectation that visible physique improvement follows automatically as a byproduct of improving the ratio of strength (muscle) to body weight over time.
