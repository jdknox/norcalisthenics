# Why fittest in the room is better than fittest in the world
2025-07-03

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qOyWb-5_9Bo) Woods poses the framing question: "Do you want to be a world-class athlete or just more capable" than the people around you?

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript.

## Core argument
- Claims the gap between an elite/professional-level athlete and a generally fit, capable person is overwhelmingly explained by two factors: (1) total time spent training consistently (attributed as roughly 90% of the difference), and (2) pushing every last percentage of possible progress via very high training volume/intensity, accepting the associated risk and time cost (the remaining ~10%) — with genetics layered on top of both.
- Central claim: reaching a high level of general fitness (strength, physique, mobility) takes a small fraction of the time required to push into that final elite tier, because of steeply diminishing returns — an initial, modest amount of consistent stimulus captures most of the available adaptation, and each additional unit of work beyond that yields progressively less.
- Illustrates with a language-learning comparison: five minutes daily over 12 weeks (total: same 5 hours) produces meaningfully better results than one five-hour session done once, since consistent smaller stimuli match how the underlying adaptation process actually works — reinforcing that total time invested matters less than consistency and effective stimulus.

## Costs of pursuing the elite tier specifically
- States that pushing for that last ~10% requires roughly 10x the time investment of reaching a generally excellent level, and additionally carries real costs beyond time: higher injury risk, greater accumulated fatigue/stress with diminishing training quality, reduced time for relationships/work/other life priorities, and — for some elite performers — performance-enhancing drug use, which he states he has no interest in.
- Frames elite performers' visible results as resulting first from consistent training over a very long period (which anyone can replicate the effects of, at a lower level, with far less time) and only secondarily from the additional intensive work that separates them from "very fit."

## His own framing
- Describes his own physique as the product of 10 years of consistent training, but built using only ~40 minutes/week — explicitly states faster progress might have been possible by training many more hours per week, but that this would have come at the cost of time spent on other parts of life he valued.
- Proposes a three-part model of overall wellbeing (physical health, quality relationships, meaningful work) and argues that pursuing elite-level physical performance specifically often reduces capacity for the other two — a trade-off he considers worthwhile only for people who have deliberately chosen competitive/elite performance as their overriding goal, not as a default approach to general fitness.
- Concludes that for people who want a strong, capable, good-looking body as one part of a broader life (rather than as a singular focus), a "best in the room" (not "best in the world") standard — achievable in well under an hour a week — is the more rational target, since it captures most of the practical benefit while preserving time and energy for everything else.
