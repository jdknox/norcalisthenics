# How To Train At Maximum Intensity Without Getting Hurt
2025-04-28

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2lOaw0oJc-s) Woods names the real obstacle: "the biggest thing I see holding people back from doing this" isn't age or ability — it's fear of injury.

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript.

## Core argument — reframing where injury risk actually comes from
- Argues intensity itself (training at/near current strength limit) is not optional if the goal is real strength/muscle/mobility gains — past an absolute beginner level, some form of high-intensity training is unavoidable for continued progress, regardless of which method or program is used.
- Claims surrounding a hard set with lighter "warm-up" volume provides no meaningful protective benefit against injury — framing warm-ups as, at best, a marginal buffer, not a substitute for addressing the actual risk factors (below). States he personally skips warm-ups entirely.
- Identifies the two real risk factors as (1) lack of control over the training load, and (2) doing too much volume — not intensity per se.

## Risk factor 1 — lack of control over load
- Diagnosis: the common fear (getting "stuck" under near-maximal effort with no way out) is legitimate for fixed external loads like a heavy barbell, where failure can mean being physically trapped under the weight.
- Claimed solution specific to bodyweight/self-scaled training: because difficulty comes from body positioning (leaning further forward, shifting weight, etc.) rather than external load, it can be reduced from maximum to zero instantly with a simple positional shift — e.g., dropping hips back toward a fully assisted stance on a handstand pushup.
- Recommends explicitly practicing and confirming "zero" (a fully unloaded, zero-difficulty version of each movement) before ever training at higher difficulty, specifically so there's always a known, immediate fallback if a set starts to feel out of control.

## Risk factor 2 — doing too much volume
- Notes a common failure mode when switching to this method from traditional training: assuming the *same total training time* as before is appropriate, when in fact this method is claimed to be dramatically more potent per minute — so total weekly time must be cut drastically (his stated recent example: 11 minutes total working time across all six movements in a given week) rather than scaled 1:1 with prior routines.
- Reiterates that recovery, not additional stimulus, is what drives strength/muscle gains between sessions — excess volume on top of an already-maximal stimulus doesn't add benefit and instead subtracts from recovery capacity.

## Practical protocol for managing both risk factors
- One continuous set per movement, once per week — this constraint alone is presented as the main structural safeguard against excess volume, since there's no decision to make about "how many sets" beyond the single one.
- Within that one set, use the "no longer fun" stopping rule (consistent with his other content) as the practical volume-management tool.
- Reiterates that establishing the zero-difficulty fallback for each movement is a prerequisite skill to learn *before* attempting to train at higher difficulty — this is what allows genuinely maximal effort without the "stuck under too much load" scenario that would otherwise be dangerous.
