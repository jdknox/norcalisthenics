# How to reverse injury while gaining strength
2024-09-24

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wfVi7XLYIZY) Woods states the goal: "Here is how you can reverse training-related injuries like tendonitis, muscle strains" and joint pain.

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript. The "intensity over volume" principle overlaps with several other summarized videos — this one's distinct contribution is a specific two-tissue-type model explaining *why* low-intensity, high-volume training is the riskier option for injury, not the safer one.

## Core model — two tissue types with different recovery properties
- **Connective tissue** (tendons, ligaments): recovers slowly from damage, but can withstand far higher forces than muscles are capable of generating — meaning it isn't force/intensity that damages it, but insufficient recovery time relative to total volume of use.
- **Muscle tissue**: responds to and is stimulated to grow specifically by mechanical tension (near-maximal force output) — low-intensity work under-stimulates it regardless of volume.

## The core argument this model produces
- Because connective tissue is damaged by *volume* (not intensity) while muscle is only grown by *intensity* (not volume), low-intensity, high-volume training is the worst combination for injury risk: it doesn't effectively build muscle (so more of it is "needed" to compensate), and that extra volume accumulates connective-tissue stress without adequate recovery time between sessions.
- Conversely, minimal-volume, maximum-intensity training is framed as protective for connective tissue (very little total load-bearing time per week, allowing full recovery) while being maximally effective for muscle growth — the two goals (safety and effectiveness) align rather than trade off, in this model.
- Explicitly states warm-ups and stretching cannot substitute for this: connective tissue damage from excess volume is physical and can only be resolved by rest/time, not offset by a warm-up routine beforehand.

## Personal account specific to this video
- Describes his own early training years as including extensive prehab, mobility work, and warm-up routines specifically to try to prevent tendonitis, strains, and joint pain — reports these issues persisted despite that effort.
- States that after switching to minimal-volume, maximum-intensity training (his standard ~40 min/week, six-movement system) and dropping the prehab/warm-up work entirely, these injury issues resolved and haven't recurred — offered as the practical case for the two-tissue-type model above, along with similar reports from his students.

## Practical takeaway (specific framing of this video)
- Directly rejects the common assumption that high-intensity training is riskier for injury, especially for older or previously-injured people — argues the opposite: minimizing total training volume while maximizing intensity per rep is the safer approach for connective tissue specifically, not a trade-off against safety.
