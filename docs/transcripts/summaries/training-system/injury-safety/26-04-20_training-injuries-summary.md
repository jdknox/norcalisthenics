# How I fixed every training injury I've had
2026-04-20

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CKS14Br8yio) Woods sets the expectation: "at some point you're going to run into issues with your body" if you train seriously.

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript.

## Injury 1 — Torn muscle (long head of tricep)
- Cause (suspected): unusually high volume from repeated filming takes, compounded by an awkward sleeping position.
- Timeline: ~2 months completely unable to do pulling work, then ~1 month of rehab back to full strength (fully resolved by the time of filming).
- Approach:
  1. **Psychological framing** — treat the urge to "fix it immediately" as a normally-useful trait whose usual outlet is blocked, not something to fight.
  2. **Redirect training energy** — during the ~2 months unable to pull, shifted all upper-body training focus onto unaffected patterns (his pushing/planche work), making real progress there instead of stopping training entirely.
  3. **Reintroduce with lighter work** — starting around week 9, used deliberately light/easier progressions (the same ones a true beginner would use) on the affected pattern purely to promote blood flow and healing and find the current pain-free limit, rather than to build strength (strength-building normally requires near-max effort, but this phase is an explicit exception).
  - Stated subjective progression: ~60% strength at week 9, ramping to ~90% by around week 12, back to full max-effort training shortly after.
- General note: muscular tears like this are attributed to overtraining/overuse (excess volume), not to training with high intensity per se.

## Injury 2 — Tendonitis (golfer's elbow / medial epicondyle pain)
- Adjustments made (kept training instead of stopping entirely):
  1. **Technique** — minimized wrist-flexor involvement by relaxing grip and avoiding excess wrist flexion; specifically credits gymnastics rings (vs. a fixed bar) for allowing the wrist to rotate through its natural path, reducing strain.
  2. **Volume** — deliberately minimized additional pulling volume beyond what's needed to stimulate the target (prime-mover) muscles.
  3. **Intensity** — trained at whatever intensity was tolerable without excessive discomfort (which, with the above changes, didn't need to drop much).
- Added direct rehab exercise: isometric/eccentric wrist flexion against a wall (palm pressed to a wall, flexing the wrist to feel the affected tendon working), done for reps between other sets, repeated every session until resolved. Framed as promoting collagen reorganization in the tendon via direct eccentric loading — noted as an area where the underlying research is described as mixed/unclear, but effective in his personal experience.

## Injury 3 — Sternum/chest pain from a back-lever attempt
- Onset: felt during an early-career back lever attempt; resulted in sharp sternum pain that made even basic pushups impossible, persisting for weeks.
- Hypothesized cause: excess chest/pec strength built without matching pec range of motion, creating high tension at the sternum attachment (commonly associated with heavy bench pressing; in his case, back lever work).
- Resolution: the German hang (same movement described in the separate shoulder-mobility summary) — used to specifically open up pec range of motion; pain resolved as range of motion improved, and recurrences (when they threaten to reappear) are managed the same way.
- Recommends building a full German hang as a general standard for anyone doing significant pushing work, to get ahead of this issue proactively (a project of roughly a couple of months), positioned as secondary to nailing the basic strength movements first.
