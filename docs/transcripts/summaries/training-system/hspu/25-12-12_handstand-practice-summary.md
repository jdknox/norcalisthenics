# Why practicing handstands is making you worse
2025-12-12

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xA5Fx4x9-O4) Woods reflects on years of drills that got him nowhere: "the large majority of practise is actually detrimental to the outcome."

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript.

## Core argument
- Distinguishes handstand *strength* (vertical pushing capacity, specifically toward a handstand pushup) from handstand *skill* (balance/coordination) — and argues most people's practice fails because they try to build the former through the latter, which doesn't work: practicing an unstable, low-load position doesn't provide enough loading or control to drive meaningful strength/muscle gain.
- Personal account: achieved a reliable handstand only after stopping dedicated practice and focusing purely on building vertical-pushing strength (toward a handstand pushup) via his standard once-weekly, full-range training — the handstand itself then required comparatively little direct skill work.
- Framed benefit beyond the handstand itself: the strength-building route also produces broader benefits (muscle mass, leanness, shoulder health) that dedicated handstand-skill practice does not.

## Mobility component
- Core mobility requirement: full overhead shoulder flexion — without it, a "banana" shape results, undermining balance and endurance in the hold.
- States this range is usually maintained automatically by properly executed full-range vertical-push training; targeted mobility work is only needed if currently lacking this range, and is treated as a finite (few-months) project rather than ongoing work (same model as his other mobility content).
- Optional additional flexibility targets for more advanced entries: pancake flexibility (chest to floor) for straddle presses/stalls; pike flexibility (forehead to shins) for pike presses — both optional, only relevant for specific advanced entry methods.

## Skill practice — alignment and execution, once strength/mobility are in place
- **Two key positions, held simultaneously:** posterior pelvic tilt (tucking the pelvis, squeezing glutes/core), and scapular elevation with an overhead shoulder flex (cue: reaching hands toward the ceiling / "shoulders to ears").
- **Progression sequence:** practice the two positions standing → same positions lying on the floor with arms raised (active hollow-body position) → the same alignment against a wall, walking in chest-first (never back-to-the-wall) so weight shifts progressively off the feet and onto the hands → eventually a fully freestanding handstand once no weight remains on the feet.
- **Explicit warning against high-volume skill practice:** because fatigue degrades alignment and balance, continuing to practice past the point of clean form is described as training (and reinforcing) a poor handstand, not a good one — recommends short, frequent, high-quality practice sessions that stop as soon as form starts to break down, rather than grinding through fatigue.

## Entry methods (kick-ups), once a freestanding hold is achievable
- Prefers controlled presses over momentum-based kick-ups (less to control mid-air).
- Progression of press-based entries, easiest to hardest: straddle press → pike press → stalder press (most advanced); can also be trained on parallettes/parallel bars as an easier variant.

## Overall time claim
- States the entire handstand capability (strength, mobility, and skill combined) was built using only the ~3 minutes/week allotted to vertical pushing within his standard 6-movement, ~40-min/week system, plus comparatively minimal dedicated skill practice once the underlying strength/mobility were in place.
