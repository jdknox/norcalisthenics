# The fastest way to build strong shoulders at home
2025-05-05

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PwutudyK_1Y) Woods contrasts approaches: "You could do this and this and this, spend a couple hours a week" in a gym, or use his 3-minute method instead.

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript. This is the detailed, step-by-step version of the handstand pushup / vertical-push progression referenced briefly elsewhere in his content.

## Goal and rationale
- Stated end-goal movement: the freestanding handstand pushup, framed as the "north star" for vertical pushing — not the starting point, but the direction of training.
- Position: raw full-range-of-motion strength in this movement pattern is the actual prerequisite for related skills (handstands, handstand presses) — dedicated skill/balance practice for those movements is described as ineffective without this underlying strength already in place, and largely unnecessary once it is.

## Equipment
- Rings or parallettes preferred; minimum viable setup is any low elevated surface for the hands (steps, low stool) low enough to allow the head to pass below hand level, plus a separate elevated platform (chair, box) for the feet as difficulty increases.

## Establishing "zero" (baseline, full range of motion, no load)
- Standing on the floor, guide through the full range of motion using the legs for all support — full range defined by two touch points: shoulders/ears touching in the fully overhead position at the top, and wrist-to-shoulder contact at the bottom.
- Key form rule for scaling difficulty later: keep the hips up throughout (torso at roughly 45–90° to the ground) — difficulty is controlled by moving the *feet*, never by letting the hips drop, since dropping the hips pulls the movement out of the vertical-pushing plane entirely.

## Scaling difficulty upward
- The controlling variable is the percentage of bodyweight resting on the hands vs. the feet — adjusted via foot position and elevation, moving through a spectrum from all-weight-on-feet to all-weight-on-hands (full freestanding handstand pushup).
- Progression sequence for increasing hand-loading: feet on the floor near the hands → feet elevated on a platform → feet on ring straps (near-full bodyweight load) → fully freestanding (zero foot support).
- Method for finding the working difficulty level each session: start with the negative (lowering) — lean forward with hips stacked over the hands until reaching the point of being forced down; if that's not enough resistance, increase hand-loading (platform, then straps) until a genuine failure point is found.
- Once the negative failure point is found, reverse the same process on the way up (the positive): push up, find a new sticking point, and if it becomes possible to continue, escalate the difficulty (move feet back toward a harder position) to find the next sticking point — continuing this cycle of "push, get stuck, adjust" all the way through the full rep.
- Scaling down is symmetric: if control is lost or the movement becomes too hard to manage safely, step feet down through the same progression (straps → platform → floor) to immediately reduce load.

## Dosage
- One continuous set, once per week; brief pauses between positive/negative portions are fine to reset form, but it's treated as a single set overall.
- Stopping cue: same rule as the rest of his system — stop as soon as the set stops being enjoyable/motivating.

## Progress tracking
- Primary metric: how much additional hand-loading (i.e., how far along the platform → straps → freestanding progression) can be achieved on the first rep of each session, since that's when strength is highest.
- Suggested waypoints to track over time: initial ability to bear some weight on the hands from a platform → getting feet onto the ring straps for part of a rep → holding a shoulder-stand-like position at the bottom → gaining partial strap-assisted range pushing up from that position → reducing strap assistance over time → eventually achieving some unassisted range → full unassisted range of motion.

## Common mistakes flagged
- Not achieving genuine full range of motion (missing the overhead or wrist-to-shoulder touch points).
- Letting the hips drop under fatigue instead of adjusting foot position — this pulls the movement out of the vertical plane and undermines the intended strength/mobility development.

## Pairing recommendation
- Recommends training the equivalent vertical-pulling movement (one-arm chin-up progression, covered in a separately summarized video) with the same once-weekly protocol, for balanced upper-body development — doesn't need to be the same day.
