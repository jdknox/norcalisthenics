# The 2 mistakes killing my calisthenics gains
2026-09-21

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Kwf-keRh0mk) Woods sums up his rule for skill positions: "if you don't train it, you don't gain it."

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript. Follows up on his recent planche/front-lever "software" experiment (see the true-cost-of-the-planche summary) by naming two specific form gaps in his own full-ROM training that have been capping "free gains" toward those two skills.

## Core principle
- Full range of motion matters because different muscles and coordination patterns switch on at different points of a movement; training only half the range (common by default) throws away stimulus for no time savings.
- For general muscle and strength, partial-range training still works fine. But static-hold skills (planche, front lever) are different — if a specific position is never trained directly, the body never learns to coordinate the muscles needed to hold it.
- Skills like back lever, handstand press, and human flag are framed as achievable mostly through raw strength alone ("manhandling" through them), whereas planche and front-lever top-range specifically demand dedicated positional practice.

## Mistake 1 — planche pushup scapula form
- Correct planche form requires protracting (pushing forward) the scapula while keeping it depressed, under load — a specific position (heavy serratus anterior engagement) that most people, himself included, don't hit by default.
- States he hasn't been reaching this position in his ordinary horizontal-push training, and that most students and videos he reviews miss it too, since it's a naturally weak position people unconsciously avoid.
- Fix: on every planche-pushup rep, actively maximize scapula protraction and full elbow lockout at the top of the rep (and at the start of the negative) — scaling difficulty back as needed to allow it. Adds no time, just intent.

## Mistake 2 — front lever row top range
- His prior range-of-motion standard for the row stopped once the elbow passed the torso — enough for general training stimulus, but short of the wrist-to-body position needed for front lever pull-ups or touches.
- Different forearm leverage demands at that top position mean strength doesn't transfer automatically from his normal working range; found himself notably weaker there.
- Fix: same approach as the pushup — scale difficulty down enough to complete each rep reaching wrist-to-torso at the top, calling the extra reach "easy gains" precisely because it's a current weak link.
