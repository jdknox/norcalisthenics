# How to maximise weekly training progress in under 40 minutes
2025-06-05

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y_KoWUqnJuk) Woods states the core claim: "When it comes to strength training, more work does not equal more results."

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript. This is a detailed explanation of the "listen to emotional feedback to find optimal weekly volume" method referenced throughout his other content.

## Core argument
- States the limiting factor on results is not how much training is done, but how fast the body can adapt to (recover from and respond to) a training stimulus — illustrated by noting that his cumulative lifetime training time (~40 min/week over ~10 years, ~340 hours total) could be logged in a single month at 12 hours/day, yet nobody achieves 10 years of results in a month this way, showing volume itself isn't the bottleneck.
- Uses a "factory" analogy: the body can only run its muscle-building "production line" so fast; the goal is to keep that process running consistently over time (not idle, not overloaded) rather than maximizing daily input.
- Describes steeply diminishing returns on additional training volume within a session/week, while fatigue costs continue accumulating linearly — meaning past a certain point, additional volume produces close to zero extra benefit while still adding recovery debt, time cost, and injury risk.
- Frames declining motivation to train more as a meaningful physiological signal (the body "fighting back" against unproductive excess), not weakness — and argues people typically misinterpret this signal as a discipline failure, leading them to either force through it (accumulating more downside) or quit entirely (losing the productive work too).

## Two-part method for finding the right weekly volume
1. **Reduce training to one controlled variable: a single continuous max-intensity set per movement, once per week.** No multiple sets, no structured rest within the set (a second or two between reps at most) — this removes ambiguity so the only decision left is when to stop.
2. **Use the "no longer fun" rule to decide when to stop.** As soon as there's genuine reluctance to continue (not just the difficulty of pushing hard, but the specific feeling of wanting to rest rather than continue), that's treated as the signal that the point of diminishing returns has been reached for that movement that week — training is called there, not before and not with forced continuation past it.
- Explicitly states that doing more (extra sets, extra sessions) after a set is voluntarily ended undermines the method: it dilutes the intensity/quality of future sessions by degrading recovery, so any perceived "extra work" comes at the cost of next week's training quality rather than being additive.

## Weekly structure recommendation (for those splitting training across days)
- Suggested split: horizontal push/pull one day, legs another day, vertical push/pull 3–4 days after the first session — yielding two upper-body stimulus sessions/week and one for legs, which he describes as close to ideal for balancing growth signaling against recovery.
- States sustainability outranks this "ideal" structure: if doing all six movements in a single weekly session is what actually gets done consistently, that consistency matters more than the marginal benefit of splitting sessions — and that skipping sessions entirely is worse than either option.

## Where to look for further gains
- States that once weekly volume is dialed via the above method, the main remaining lever for faster progress is training *technique* quality — specifically, actually reaching genuine maximum intensity across the *entire* range of motion of each movement (not just at one point), since incomplete range of motion or backing off intensity partway through a rep (often unconsciously) is cited as the most common mistake limiting people's results, more so than volume itself.
