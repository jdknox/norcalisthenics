# This will revolutionise how you see fitness
2025-05-20

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xzllZsPmDBQ) Woods traces the discovery back five years: "About five years ago, I stumbled upon a method of strength training" that cut his weekly time by over 80%.

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript. This video presents the clearest single statement of the theoretical case for his whole self-scaled bodyweight training method, contrasting it explicitly with fixed-external-load (barbell/machine) training.

## Diagnosed problems with fixed-resistance training (barbell, machines, standard bodyweight)
1. **A fixed load forces the set to end at failure** — once the load exceeds current strength, no further work is possible without a spotter, so genuinely maximal effort is only accumulated for a few seconds per set (his estimate: under a minute of true max-effort stimulus across an entire hour-long session).
2. **Only one point in the range of motion gets challenged to failure** — the rest of the rep, even on a "failed" set, is sub-maximal, since a fixed weight can only be tuned to be maximally hard at one sticking point.
3. **Reaching failure accumulates "junk volume" as a side effect** — the warm-up sets and sub-maximal reps needed to work up to a failure attempt add fatigue/wear to joints and connective tissue without contributing much stimulus, creating a trade-off between accumulating enough effective stimulus and accumulating unproductive damage.
4. **Failure with a fixed external load is a safety/control issue** — being stuck under a heavy barbell mid-rep requires a spotter or carries real risk, unlike a self-limited bodyweight load.

## The "ideal" method, and how bodyweight training achieves it
- Describes a hypothetical ideal: resistance that's already at maximum difficulty from the very first rep, continuously adjustable to stay maximally challenging as a person fatigues or moves through a range of motion, and always instantly reducible to zero — theorized to concentrate an entire session's worth of effective stimulus into a few continuous minutes.
- States self-scaled bodyweight training via body positioning (rather than external weight) achieves this: because the "load" is the person's own leverage on their bodyweight, it can be adjusted continuously and instantly, including down to zero, at any point in a rep.

## The six movements and their "difficulty knob" (brief version — full mechanics covered in his other movement-specific videos)
- Horizontal push (plange pushup): forward lean distance between shoulders and hands.
- Horizontal pull (front lever row): distance the rings are pushed away toward the feet.
- Vertical push (handstand pushup): how much bodyweight is shifted onto the hands (via foot position/elevation).
- Vertical pull (one-arm chin-up): how much assistance is given via the other arm/feet.
- Squat (single-leg squat): external load (weight vest) plus hand/other-foot assistance.
- Knee flexion (Nordic curl): amount of hand assistance used.
- In each case, the "knob" is dialed to find and stay at the current maximum-effort threshold throughout the rep, rather than using a single fixed difficulty for the whole set.

## Resulting protocol and claimed time savings
- Train each of the six movements to genuine failure across its full range of motion, once per week, stopping when it's no longer enjoyable (same stopping rule used throughout his content).
- Claims this concentrates roughly the same effective training stimulus as a multi-hour traditional program into an estimated 10–20 minutes/week of actual working time (2–3 minutes per movement) — with proportionally less accumulated "junk volume" wear, since the low-value work is simply not done rather than being trained around.
- Split recommendation: same as elsewhere in his content — movements can be paired across 3 sessions/week (or done in one longer session if needed), with the total time for either approach staying under roughly 40 minutes/week including rest.
