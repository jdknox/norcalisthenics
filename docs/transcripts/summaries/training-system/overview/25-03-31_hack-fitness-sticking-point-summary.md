# How to hack fitness and build the body of your dreams in 40min a week
2025-03-31

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tr7IvT_DndM) Woods opens with a challenge: "If training for more than 40 minutes a week was going to get me better results," he'd do it — this video explains why it wouldn't.

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript. This is his fullest explanation of the "sticking point" / forced-negatives concept that underlies his self-scaled bodyweight training method, with demonstrations across all six movements.

## Core concept — finding and training the "sticking point"
- Frames maximum strength as a threshold: below it, movement is possible; at or above it, the person gets physically stuck (a "sticking point") — the exact moment of getting stuck is, by definition, working at 100% of current capacity.
- Central claim: in traditional training, this sticking point is reached for only a couple of seconds per set (typically right at the end, if at all) — the overwhelming majority of training time is spent below this threshold and therefore contributes comparatively little to strength/muscle stimulus.
- The core technique: rather than building up to one brief sticking point per set, deliberately find and work *at* the sticking point continuously — get stuck, back off difficulty just enough to move an inch further, get stuck again, repeat all the way through the full range of motion. This is described as compressing what would normally be scattered seconds of true effective stimulus (spread across many traditional sets) into one continuous, maximally effective set.

## Demonstrated across each movement (brief mechanics; full protocols covered in his movement-specific videos)
- **Nordic curl:** attempt unassisted from the very start (this is itself the sticking point for most people) — add just enough hand assistance to move an inch, remove it, fail again, repeat up through the full range.
- **Horizontal pull (front lever row):** push the rings away (toward the feet) until rowing becomes impossible, work against that resistance, ease off slightly to gain an inch, then reload the resistance and repeat, working up toward feet leaving the ground and eventually a front lever.
- **Vertical pull (one-arm chin-up):** start by attempting a full one-arm chin-up (the end goal itself) as the sticking point, using feet assistance only enough to gain each successive inch, building over time toward needing less assistance and eventually none.
- **Vertical push (handstand pushup) and squat/leg movements:** same "get stuck → back off minimally → get stuck again" cycle applied via foot position/loading, as detailed in his separately summarized movement-specific videos.

## Key execution mistakes flagged
1. **Stopping after hitting the first sticking point**, mistaking it for failure/inability rather than recognizing it as the intended starting point of effective training — the fix is to back off difficulty just enough to keep moving, not to quit.
2. **Doing far more total volume than this method calls for** (e.g. attempting many reps/sets as with traditional training) — since each rep now contains the equivalent effective stimulus of many traditional sets, total volume needs to be dramatically reduced, not matched to prior training habits.
3. **Rushing through the range of motion** instead of treating each small increment of range as its own "sticking point" to find and work against individually — rushing skips the deliberate re-scaling needed at each point in the range and reduces overall effectiveness.

## Programming
- One set per movement per week, with total time per movement in the range of ~2–3 minutes; across all six movements, a combined weekly working-time estimate of roughly 12–18 minutes (paired across 2–3 sessions if splitting the week, or done in one longer session if needed).
- Stopping rule: same "as soon as it's no longer fun, you're done" cue used throughout his content.
- Reiterates that progress is guaranteed by consistently testing for slightly more difficulty each week (a bit more forward lean, a bit less assistance, a bit more range) — technique execution (finding and working the sticking point properly) matters more than any other detail of programming.
