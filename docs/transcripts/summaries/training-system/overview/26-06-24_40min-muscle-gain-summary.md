# Is 40min a week enough for muscle gain?
2026-06-24

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6eKoCmBM7mI) Woods acknowledges that "research consistently shows that higher volume training produces faster strength and muscle gain."

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript.

## Core science referenced
- Growth driver: mechanical tension at the muscle-fiber level — the "grinding," near-failure portion of a rep/set is what signals growth; work that never approaches failure doesn't stimulate growth regardless of total volume.
- His existing method: skip the sub-maximal build-up of a traditional set and start each set already at/near failure — accumulating "effective reps" continuously rather than via multiple traditional sets to failure.

## The missing piece: central nervous system (CNS) fatigue
- Force output depends on two things: the muscle's raw capacity, and the CNS's ability to signal high-threshold motor units (the largest, most growth-responsive fibers) to fire.
- CNS fatigue accumulates quickly and disproportionately reduces signaling to exactly those high-threshold motor units — so continuing to "grind" past a certain point can leave you doing low-value work on small, endurance-oriented fibers while feeling like you're still working hard.
- Personal anecdote: adding an extra set on top of his usual training caused severe fatigue and poor performance — attributed to CNS fatigue, not muscular fatigue.
- Two fatigue timescales noted: local/CNS fatigue that can dissipate within ~5 minutes within a session, and a longer-lasting systemic fatigue that can persist for multiple days (illustrated by a period of missed training followed by unusually strong performance).

## New experiment / protocol
- Restructuring sets as short bursts of near-max effort (cut off before failure-driven CNS drop-off), separated by ~5-minute rests, alternating between two antagonist movements (e.g. horizontal push / horizontal pull) so each movement gets a genuine recovery window between attempts.
- Example structure: ~30 seconds near-max effort on movement A, ~2 min rest, ~30 seconds on movement B, ~2 min rest, repeat — approaching 5 minutes before returning to movement A again; this "unit" can be repeated as many times as desired.
- Set count being tested: as many as 5 repetitions of this cycle in early testing, expecting to settle closer to 3 (broadly matching typical 3–5-set recommendations in the literature).
- Stopping rule (revised): stop once performance/output visibly drops below your best that session — not once it merely becomes psychologically hard.
- Time cost: even the higher end of this (5x cycles) totals roughly 23 minutes per session, ×3 sessions/week ≈ just over an hour/week; a 3-set version runs closer to 13 min/session (~40 min/week total, similar to his original time budget).
- Early subjective observations: performance in sets 2–4 sometimes felt stronger than set 1 (attributed to being "warmed up"); movements with naturally longer/slower single-rep execution (one-arm chin-ups, planche pushups, single-leg squats) felt harder to repeat for multiple sets than faster movements (front levers, handstand pushups).

## Explicit framing
- States this optimization is secondary — the fundamentals (consistency, intensity, and technique on the basic once-weekly movements) still account for the large majority of results; frames additional-set experimentation as marginal, not a prerequisite.
