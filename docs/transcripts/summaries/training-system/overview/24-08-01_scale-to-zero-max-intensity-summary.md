# Core training philosophy: scale to zero, train at max intensity, minimal volume
Four overlapping posts/videos, combined here since they cover the same core argument from different angles:
- "How to start calisthenics as a beginner (Avoid this mistake!)" — 2024-08-21 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mF8pp3lW__A
- "How to make 40min of training more effective than 5 hours" — 2024-08-01 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NnOgevQKlY0
- "How To Go From Weak To Strong As Directly As Possible" — 2023-09-07 (blog post; demo video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lVkYP_09wN8)
- "You Already Know The Perfect Training Volume" — 2023-08-16 (blog post, no video)

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript, of the shared argument across all four, with notes on what's distinct to each.

## The shared core argument
- **The key mistake most beginners make:** confusing "low absolute difficulty" (fine — everyone starts somewhere) with "low relative intensity" (a waste of time). Training below current maximum strength capacity doesn't stimulate growth regardless of how much of it is done.
- **"Everything scales to zero":** every movement in his six-movement system can be adjusted, via body positioning, all the way from elite end-goal difficulty down to requiring no strength whatsoever — so there's no strength prerequisite to start training any of them "properly." The method for an absolute beginner and a 10-year advanced trainee is identical; only the absolute difficulty level differs.
- **Practical implication:** rather than training separate "beginner exercises" that are too easy to build toward an end goal, scale the *actual* goal movement down to a difficulty that's currently just barely achievable, and train that. As strength increases, the difficulty is scaled back up — so the exercise itself never has to change, only its current difficulty setting.
- **Why traditional (fixed-resistance) training is inefficient:** a barbell or machine holds one fixed weight, so only a small fraction of a set or session ever reaches genuine maximal effort (the rest is sub-maximal "junk volume" that still accumulates fatigue/joint stress without much growth benefit). Self-scaled bodyweight training can instead start at failure from the first centimeter of the first rep and stay there.
- **How much volume is "enough":** rather than target rep/set counts (which encourage subconsciously pacing effort to last the whole prescribed volume), the recommended approach is one continuous set per movement, per week, at true maximal effort, stopping only once genuine 100% effort can no longer be sustained — normally 1–4 reps and a couple of minutes. Doing more than this doesn't add benefit; it just dilutes intensity and adds fatigue without added growth signal.

## What's distinct to each post
- **"Start calisthenics as a beginner"** frames this most explicitly as a two-step beginner action plan: (1) scale the actual end-goal movements down to currently-trainable difficulty, (2) never make it easier than strictly necessary — states this alone is what separates fast results from his own first five years of beginner-to-intermediate stagnation despite heavy weekly training volume.
- **"40min more effective than 5 hours"** is the personal-history version of the argument: describes his own multi-year plateau doing traditional high-volume training, the lockdown-driven switch to bodyweight training, and the gradual realization (cutting from ~10-20 sets/week down to 1 set/movement/week) that training quality improved as volume decreased — framing this as a "leap of faith" moment.
- **"Weak to strong as directly as possible"** is the shortest, most direct statement of the "everything scales to zero" principle, explicitly aimed at people who feel intimidated by advanced-looking movements (rings, planches, one-arm chin-ups) — reassures that even zero perceptible load (hands not even touching the rings) is a valid, scaled starting point, and that weaker/more untrained people should expect *faster* initial results precisely because they're further from their genetic ceiling.
- **"You already know the perfect training volume"** is the most detailed argument against traditional set/rep prescriptions specifically — argues that a fixed volume target itself undermines intensity (subconsciously pacing effort to "have enough left" for remaining sets), and that listening to the body's own signal (continuing exactly as long as genuine 100% effort feels good/wanted, then stopping) is a more accurate volume regulator than any programmed number.
