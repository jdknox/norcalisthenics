# How to actually know if your calisthenics is working
2026-06-15

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UwOxV1oYeO0) Woods warns that without tracking, "progressing in callisthenics is kind of like driving blind."

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript.

## Why not to count reps
- Rep count creates a perverse incentive: to hit higher numbers, people unconsciously reduce intensity/range of motion (his example: kipping pull-ups) — the opposite of what builds strength.
- Since higher intensity inherently produces fewer reps (an inverse relationship with the stated goal of counting reps), rep-counting works against genuine strength progress.

## What to track instead: your "current frontier"
- For any bodyweight movement there's a continuous difficulty spectrum from beginner to elite; wherever you currently sit on that spectrum for a given progression is your "frontier."
- The training that matters is done right at that frontier (current max difficulty) — easier work doesn't drive progress.
- Practical method: at the start of each session, while fresh, test/attempt your current frontier for that movement and use that as this session's data point; watch it move up over time as the actual measure of progress (analogous to tracking a 1-rep-max in barbell training).

3. **Feet-elevated pike pushups**: frontier = how much of the positive range of motion you can complete with feet elevated and hips kept up (a hip drop invalidates the rep).
4. **Shoulder-stand on rings** (full bodyweight loaded, using strap assistance as needed): frontier = how much you can push up out of this position, using the straps to assist as needed; the hardest part of the movement is roughly mid-range.
5. Passing the sticking point unlocks the ability to attempt the full bodyweight handstand pushup using strap assistance.
6. **Unassisted negatives**: frontier = how far down you can control the descent without any strap assistance, working toward a full controlled negative.
7. **Unassisted positive**: frontier = how much range you can press back up unassisted — the end-goal-level frontier.
8. Beyond the raw movement: for advanced practitioners, the frontier can shift to quality metrics — strict form, balance without weight-shifting, multiple clean reps — rather than just raw range of motion.

## Worked example - front lever row progression and its frontiers
1. **Bodyweight row** (all weight still on the feet): frontier = walking the feet back far enough to load the row while keeping all weight on the feet.
2. **Extended bodyweight row**: push the rings away toward the feet, increasing the horizontal distance from shoulders to hands; frontier = the greatest distance you can reach for some range of motion.
3. **Tuck front lever row**: frontier = getting the feet off the ground with knees tucked to the chest, for any range of motion.
4. **Level-hip tuck row**: frontier = completing some range of the row with hips level with the shoulders, before having to pull the hips up.
5. **Extended tuck row**: frontier = how far you can extend out while keeping the hips level; tuck back into a ball once that position can no longer be maintained, then keep training.
6. **Advanced-tuck front lever row** (90 degrees at the hip): frontier = achieving that body shape for some range of motion.
7. **Full-lay front lever row**: frontier = achieving a full front-lever body line for some range of motion; this is the end-goal-level frontier.

## Worked example - planche pushup progression and its frontiers
1. **Kneeling pushup**: frontier = a full-range rep with the knees down and all weight removed from the hands; this is the system's zero-load starting point.
2. **Feet-down forward-lean pushup**: frontier = the greatest horizontal distance you can create between shoulders and hands on the first rep, while the feet stay on the floor. This is Woods's primary PPU progress metric.
3. **Tuck planche pushup**: frontier = getting the feet off the ground with knees tightly tucked to the chest, for any range of motion; hips do not yet need to be level with the shoulders.
4. **Level-hip tuck pushup**: frontier = completing some range of motion with hips level with the shoulders, before they drop.
5. **Extended tuck pushup**: frontier = how far you can open the hips from that level-hip position without losing hip height; tuck back into a ball once the level position is lost, then keep training.
6. **Advanced-tuck planche pushup** (90 degrees at the hip): frontier = achieving that body shape for some range of motion.
7. **Full-lay planche pushup**: frontier = achieving a full body line for some range of motion. The bottom is mechanically easiest; this is the bent-arm-planche end-goal frontier.

For a level-up, Woods only requires touching the next criterion for some range of motion on the first, freshest rep; it does not need to be a complete rep or a sustained hold. Afterward, scale back to lower variations as needed to continue full-range, max-effort training.

## Takeaway
- Identify which progression you're currently using for each movement pattern, identify your current frontier within it, and make pushing that frontier the sole objective each session — once a progression is achieved with full range of motion, move to the next harder progression.
