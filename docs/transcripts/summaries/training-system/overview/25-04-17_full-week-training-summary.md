# Full week of training – How to get strong in 40 minutes
2025-04-17

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JSmaJS4-vl4) Woods states his baseline directly: "I have trained for less than 40 minutes a week for over five years" to build his current physique — this video is a full walkthrough of that week.

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript. This is an uncut, narrated demonstration of a full training week rather than new theory — useful mainly for the specific execution details called out during the demo.

## General execution notes across all six movements
- No warm-up performed — goes directly into working sets, all-out effort from the first rep.
- Primary progress metric: how much strength/difficulty is demonstrated on the very first rep of each movement's set, since that's the point of least fatigue and therefore the clearest signal of current maximum strength.
- Explicitly states that "clean," consistent-looking reps are a bad sign in his coaching — if a student can maintain the same difficulty level throughout a set without needing to adjust, it means they're training below their actual limit; messy, constantly-adjusted reps (switching progression level rep to rep) are the expected and desired appearance of genuinely maximal training.
- Confirms the three things that matter for each rep: correct plane/direction of motion, full range of motion, and maximum difficulty at every point within that range (both on the way up and the way down) — nothing else about form "prettiness" matters.
- Positive and negative portions of a rep are trained at *different* difficulty levels deliberately: the negative (lowering) is made heavier than the positive (lifting) was, since reversing direction requires overcoming a load that was just barely liftable — reinforces that going lighter isn't correct for the negative.

## Session-specific observations from the walkthrough
- **Horizontal push (planche pushup) and horizontal pull (front lever row), same day:** roughly 5 minutes' rest taken between the two movements (used as a natural pairing, though a full rest day between movements is equally valid if preferred).
- **Legs (single-leg squat), a separate day:** notes training this after a tiring physical activity earlier that day (surfing) — general point being that showing up and working at whatever the day's actual maximum is remains valid and effective, rather than needing "fresh" conditions every time.
- **Nordic curl:** mentions no fixed order of positive/negative reps — the choice of which to prioritize first (while freshest) depends on which specific aspect of the movement is currently being tracked for progress.
- **Vertical push (handstand pushup) and vertical pull (one-arm chin-up), final day:** notes starting directly with a few partial-range reps at the current "frontier" (hardest achievable progression) specifically to capture a clean strength-progress data point before broader-range training continues.

## Strength testing for unilateral movements
- Notes that one-arm chin-ups and single-leg squats lack the same clear intermediate "progression levels" as bilateral movements, making pure training-set data harder to interpret as pure progress — recommends a brief separate strength test (e.g. partial-range one-arm chin-up, or a specific single-leg squat variant) at the start of the relevant session specifically to generate a trackable data point, distinct from the main training work that follows.

## Stated totals
- Measured total active working time across the full week (all six movements): 11 minutes.
- Notes that even accounting for setup, rest between movements, and doing everything back-to-back in one session (the least time-efficient way to run it), total weekly time stays under 40 minutes.
