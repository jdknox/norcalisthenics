# The most potent training technique known to man
2025-03-13

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s3Y8RABDuv0) Woods states plainly: "I tell people I do less than 45 minutes a week of structured exercise" — and this technique is why.

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript. This is his fullest dedicated explanation of "forcing the negative," a concept referenced throughout his other content — the core idea overlaps heavily with his other videos, so this summary focuses on the specific mechanism and numbers unique to this one.

## Core technique
- "Forcing the negative": rather than lowering a rep under control, deliberately increasing difficulty at the top of a rep until the lowering (eccentric) phase becomes impossible to resist — the person is physically forced down despite maximal effort to keep pushing/pulling up.
- Rationale: cites research suggesting people are roughly 40% stronger eccentrically (lowering) than concentrically (lifting) — meaning a standard "lower under control" negative, even one following a maximal positive, is typically far below true eccentric capacity and therefore under-stimulating.
- Claims force negatives are the single heaviest, most potent portion of his training — the point where he sees the clearest week-to-week strength progress, and where his highest-level progressions are used.

## Demonstrated example — planche pushup
- At the top of a pushup (elbows slightly bent), progressively increase forward lean (shoulders further in front of hands) until the body starts to lower involuntarily despite maximal resistance — that point is "forcing the negative."
- Frames all six of his system's movements as having one adjustable difficulty variable each (detailed in his movement-specific videos) that can be used the same way: increased until the person is forced through the negative rather than lowering voluntarily.

## Framing and practical note
- Explicitly warns this method is very taxing: states that in his testing, most people cap out at under 20 minutes total of this style of training per week across all movements combined — consistent with his standard once-weekly, "stop when it's no longer fun" protocol described throughout his other content.
- Notes that because strength naturally increases over time, the amount of difficulty needed to force a negative each week will also increase — meaning progressive overload happens automatically as a byproduct of continuing to apply this technique, without needing to separately plan for it.
