# The most effective training routine
2026-04-11

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QKdoRXZ8W-k) Woods states his baseline: "One session a week, 40 minutes of intense proper strength training" is enough to transform your body — but there's a faster option.

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript.

## Two factors governing how to split training across the week
1. **Muscle protein synthesis (MPS):** cited as staying elevated for roughly 48 hours after training a muscle group.
2. **Fatigue:** cited as mostly dissipating roughly 72 hours after a session (varies with total workload and individual recovery factors).
- Combined implication: hitting a given muscle group roughly every 3–4 days (i.e., about twice a week over a 7-day week) is presented as the sweet spot — capturing repeated MPS windows while allowing adequate fatigue recovery between sessions on that same muscle group.

## Two types of fatigue to manage when structuring sessions
- **Localized fatigue:** movements sharing prime movers (e.g. horizontal push and vertical push both heavily use chest/shoulders/triceps) shouldn't be trained back-to-back in the same session — doing so means the second movement is trained well below max intensity. Solution: separate same-pattern-group movements (the two pushes, the two pulls) by ~3–4 days.
- **General/systemic (CNS-level) fatigue:** accumulates across the whole body regardless of which specific muscles are worked. His practical guideline (based on personal experience, not cited research): 2 movements per session allows genuinely maximal effort on both; 3 is workable; going to 4–6 movements in one session progressively degrades intensity on later movements and increases the psychological barrier to starting the session at all.

## His default/recommended routine (3 sessions/week)
- **Day 1 (e.g. Monday):** horizontal push + horizontal pull (antagonist pair — no interference between them) — roughly 10 minutes total including a ≥5-minute rest between the two movements.
- **Day 2 (e.g. Thursday or Friday):** vertical push + vertical pull — placed 3–4 days after Day 1 to satisfy the fatigue-management window on push/pull muscle groups.
- **Day 3 (any day, placement flexible):** legs (squat + knee-flexion pattern together) — kept on its own day so general/systemic fatigue from leg training doesn't interfere with upper-body sessions.

## Alternative splits (with stated trade-offs)
- **2 sessions/week (full-body each):** squats added to the first upper-body day, Nordic curls added to the second — used by Woods himself while traveling for convenience (fewer separate training days needed). Trade-off: more general fatigue per session, sessions take longer, and each movement suffers somewhat versus the 3-day version — expect to feel "wrecked" for 30–60 minutes after.
- **1 session/week (all 6 movements at once):** works as an occasional solution (e.g. before a period without training access) but with amplified versions of the same trade-offs — deliberately not recommended as a default.
- **6 sessions/week (1 movement per day):** explicitly considered and rejected as not worth the added adherence burden — antagonist pairing with adequate rest is judged to already prevent meaningful interference within a session.

## Legs frequency
- States that training the squat/knee-flexion pair once a week is sufficient for his own goals; strength/muscle aren't judged to meaningfully regress within a week of no training. Cites two reasons for keeping legs to once-weekly rather than the twice-weekly upper-body cadence: (1) his own priority is upper-body development, and (2) squat training in particular is described as very fatiguing, so a full week of recovery is preferred for training quality and long-term adherence. Notes a twice-weekly leg split is possible but not recommended until the basic once-weekly routine is already being run consistently with clear ongoing progress.
