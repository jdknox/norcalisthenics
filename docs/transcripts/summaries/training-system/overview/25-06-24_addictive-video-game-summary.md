# How to turn fitness into an addictive video game
2025-06-24

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DQs3IE0dgpw) Woods explains the stakes: "Being a beginner is the easiest time to get fast results in anything" — but it doesn't always feel that way.

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript. **Note:** this is the source video for the detailed 7-level progression tables already on file in this project's `woods_levels.md` — that file covers the level criteria for all six movement patterns (plus the lower-body patterns) in full. This summary instead focuses on the framing/motivation argument around the levels system, which `woods_levels.md` doesn't cover.

## Core problem being addressed
- Early-stage training/skill progress often feels insignificant relative to the size of a multi-year end goal, which he identifies as the point of highest dropout risk — people lose motivation and quit specifically because they lack a way to recognize incremental progress as real progress.
- Proposes a discrete "levels" system (1–7, beginner to elite) as the fix: converting an otherwise vague, slow-feeling multi-year journey into a series of concrete, achievable, visibly-marked milestones — explicitly modeled on video-game progression/leveling for its motivational effect.

## How the system is meant to be used (methodology, not the specific level criteria)
- For each of the six basic movement patterns, identify current level by testing against the defined criteria (full criteria in `woods_levels.md`), then treat the *next* level up as the sole near-term target for that movement — long-term end goals stay in the background, but day-to-day focus stays on the next achievable step.
- Clarifies that hitting a level's criterion only requires reaching it for *some* range of motion on the first, freshest rep of a session — not completing a full rep at that level, and not sustaining it for an entire set (completing a full rep easily is treated as a sign that progression level is already too easy, i.e. it's time to move up).
- Notes it's normal and expected to be at different levels across different movement patterns simultaneously — the six don't need to progress in lockstep.
- Recommends writing down current levels and tracking new ones as they're unlocked, treating each level-up as worth deliberately recognizing/celebrating, specifically to reinforce the sense of tangible progress that keeps early-stage motivation intact.

## Bonus/unlock framing
- Frames certain "fun" skills (muscle-ups, handstands, back levers, and others — see `woods_levels.md`'s "Bonus unlocks" section for the specific level thresholds) as automatic byproducts of reaching certain levels on the underlying strength movements, rather than things requiring separate dedicated practice — positioned as an added motivational hook within the leveling framework, not a new training requirement.

## Underlying execution principle (stated once, applies throughout)
- Regardless of which level a person is training at, the method is the same: push with maximum intensity across the full currently-accessible range of motion, self-scaling difficulty as described throughout his other content — the level criteria describe *what* to test for, not a different way of training at each stage.
