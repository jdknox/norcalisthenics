# How to unlock a deep squat without stretching more
2026-01-23

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=S0RcD7skLhA) Woods reframes common squat faults: "you don't have a flexibility problem. You have a strength training problem."

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript.

## Core principle
- Common squat faults (falling backward, heel lifting, knees rolling forward) are attributed to strength-training setup errors, not a lack of flexibility — and are claimed to be fixable through correctly loaded single-leg squat training rather than dedicated mobility drills.
- Same nervous-system/strength model as his other mobility content: building strength in the deepest currently-accessible range of a joint (e.g. ankle flexion) permanently improves comfort/access to that range — separate mobility work becomes unnecessary once this training is set up correctly.

## Two things to get right
1. **Keep the load centered over the working foot.** If weight shifts off-center, the working leg never actually bears (and pushes against) the resistance, so no strength or mobility stimulus is produced regardless of how much work is done.
2. **Train through the full currently-accessible range of motion**, starting from maximum hip/knee/ankle flexion — missing part of the range both reduces the strength/muscle stimulus and specifically prevents the ankle-mobility improvement the deep position is meant to drive.

## Fixing the two common failure patterns
- **Heel stays flat, weight shifts backward:** happens when ankle mobility isn't sufficient to sit centered — takes the load off-center and prevents genuine effort/progress.
- **Heel rolls forward, hips/knee drop out of position:** avoids the ankle-mobility demand but sacrifices a large portion of hip-extension range, undermining glute development and adding knee strain.
- **Fix for both, if ankle mobility is currently limiting:** elevate the heel (a wedge, board, or stacked books) just enough to allow full hip flexion with the weight centered over the foot, while reducing the ankle-flexion angle required — this lets training begin productively at any starting mobility level.
- Support setup: two sturdy hip-height platforms/rails, one on each side, so balance is removed from the movement entirely, allowing focus purely on load, position, and range of motion; use zero load initially if needed, guiding through the range with hands/other leg as needed.

## Loading and progression
- Load example given: 60 kg used currently; suggested starting range for students is 20–40 kg, increasing once too much of the range becomes easy.
- As strength increases, progressively reduce the amount of heel elevation used, working toward eventually squatting with the heel flat on the ground through full range.
- Once heel elevation is no longer needed, the stated ongoing focus is purely continued strength progression toward 75% bodyweight loaded on a single-leg squat (referenced as the same end goal detailed in the six-movements summary) — mobility is described as self-maintaining from that point via the strength training itself.

## Optional supplementary drills (for the deepest end range specifically), roughly in order of progression
1. Deep double-leg squat hold, using an external anchor to pull into the position (working toward feet-together, heels-down, full knee bend).
2. Transition from a double-leg squat into a single-leg balance hold at end range, keeping the heel down and hips fully flexed.
3. A controlled single-leg squat negative, finishing in a balanced bottom position — once achievable, heel elevation is no longer needed in regular training.
