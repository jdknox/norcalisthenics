# How to build powerful legs in one rep a week
2025-05-29

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tmb7zNxKaRQ) Woods identifies the turning point: "There was one key moment in my training career that utterly changed" his physique — starting consistent leg training.

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript. This is the fullest single-video explanation of his loaded single-leg-squat method (also referenced in several other summarized videos).

## Why he'd avoided leg training, and why it mattered
- States years of strong upper-body training and good nutrition alone weren't enough to reach his target body composition — leg training specifically, once made consistent, was what he credits for the final breakthrough (reporting the change within about a year of starting).
- Reasoning: legs carry a large share of total body muscle mass, so under a calorie deficit without adequate leg training, the body tends to lose muscle broadly (getting "smaller" rather than "leaner") rather than preferentially losing fat — undermining the goal of a lean, defined look regardless of upper-body work.
- Identifies two prior barriers: traditional leg training being physically exhausting/time-consuming and seemingly gym-dependent, and simply trying to do too much of it — the actual fix, in his account, was reducing the commitment to the smallest sustainable amount (starting at one single rep/week) rather than a mindset or willpower change.

## Why one movement pattern covers "squats and deadlifts" combined
- Standard barbell training separates knee-extension-dominant work (squats/leg press, quad-focused) from hip-extension-dominant work (deadlifts/hip thrusts, glute-focused) because a fixed external load can only be made maximally challenging at one sticking point in the range of motion — usually the bottom (knee-extension-limited), leaving the top (hip-extension-limited) under-stimulated.
- Because a self-scaled single-leg squat can be made maximally difficult continuously through the entire range (not just one sticking point), it's argued to cover both knee-extension and hip-extension strength/muscle development in one movement pattern, without needing separate exercises.

## Protocol
### Progression to zero-load, full range of motion
- Standard used for full range: standing to a fully bent knee at the bottom (no gap between hamstring and calf), with as much hip flexion as possible (knee toward chest) — heel allowed to lift if needed to achieve this depth.
- Balance check: remove the assisting hands mid-rep — falling straight down means the weight is properly centered over the foot; falling backward indicates the weight needs to shift further forward (heel lift is an acceptable way to achieve this).

### Adding load
- Method: a weighted vest (stackable for increasing load), assisted by hands on two sturdy side platforms and/or the other foot as needed through the hardest (bottom) portion of the range.
- Key principle: use enough weight that assistance is still needed through as much of the range as possible — the rep is effectively "done" (no longer providing stimulus) at the exact point where the working leg alone can complete the rest of the movement unassisted, since everything past that point is sub-maximal.
- Practical technique for finding the true limit throughout the rep: periodically test by removing the assisting hand/foot during the ascent — failing/needing to reapply assistance repeatedly is expected and desirable (functions as built-in forced negatives).
- Example load cited: 60 kg (~75% of his ~80 kg bodyweight), used with assistance through roughly the lower half of the range.

## Dosage
- One set, once per week; effort at/near failure from the very first rep, minimal rest between reps (brief pauses to reset form/balance are fine).
- Stopping cue: as soon as it's no longer enjoyable/motivating to continue — often just one rep, occasionally two.
- States this has been his actual practice for most of his training history — one rep/week has been sufficient for the results shown.

## Safety note
- Explicitly argues against injury concerns for this style of training: reasons that connective tissue (tendons) can tolerate substantially more force than the muscles attached to them can actually generate, so a person isn't strong enough to injure themselves this way purely through their own maximum muscular effort in a controlled range of motion — frames undertraining and resulting weakness, not high-intensity training itself, as the larger long-term injury risk.

## Progress tracking
- Suggested pre-set test sequence to track underlying strength before adding load: two-leg squat → negative single-leg squat → positive single-leg squat → progressively loaded single-leg squats in increments (10 kg, 20 kg, 30 kg, 40 kg…).
- Stated long-term benchmark: single-leg squat loaded to 75% of bodyweight (consistent with the goal referenced in his other content).

## Acknowledged gap
- Notes this protocol covers knee/hip extension (the squat/deadlift-equivalent pattern) but not hip flexion training specifically; states he doesn't train hip flexion directly and doesn't have a self-scaled solution for it, citing Ben Patrick's ("Knees Over Toes Guy") work as the closest existing resource for that gap.
