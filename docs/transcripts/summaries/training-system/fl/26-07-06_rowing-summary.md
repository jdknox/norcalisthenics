# You're not rowing hard enough
2026-07-06

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JcS6_llzbBg) Woods opens with the diagnosis: "most people's upper backs are weak, leaving them slouch forward" into a hunched posture.

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript.

## Diagnosis
- Weak upper back → forward-slouched posture; standard fixes (postural cueing, stretching) don't address the underlying strength deficit.
- Core problem with typical rows (light dumbbell rows, bodyweight rows): the load never approaches true strength limit at any point in the rep, so no growth signal is sent — regardless of total volume, frequency, or years of training. Adding more sub-maximal volume doesn't fix this, and can make it harder to later reach genuine intensity.

## The force-curve problem
- A fixed external load only challenges you maximally at one specific point in the range (wherever your leverage is weakest) — everywhere else in the rep is sub-maximal.
- Too light → never challenges the limit anywhere in the range (no growth stimulus). Too heavy → you can complete only the easiest part of the range before hitting failure, so you again don't get a full-range maximal stimulus.

## Solution — self-scaling bodyweight row
- Uses a "difficulty knob": resistance is adjusted continuously through the rep by changing the horizontal distance between the shoulders and the hands (how far the hands/rings are pushed out toward the feet) — this approximates matching resistance to current strength at every point in the range, keeping effort near-maximal throughout.
- Execution: from the top of a bodyweight row (rings, TRX, a table, a sheet over a door, or parallel bars), progressively push the hands away (increasing the shoulder-to-hand lever) until forced into a controlled negative all the way to full elbow lock-out at the bottom; on the way back up, continuously ease the lever distance just enough to keep pulling at maximal effort the whole way.
- To make it easier: walk the feet back in (shortens the lever). To make it harder: push feet further out, eventually lifting them off the ground.
- Progression target: first goal is getting the feet off the ground into a tuck front lever row; from there, a straightforward incremental path toward a full front lever (see the front-lever-row progression in `woods_levels.md`).

## Dosage
- As little as ~3 reps/week (his stated actual usage), 1 set, stopped as soon as maximal effort can no longer be sustained.
- Optionally add a second pulling session (a vertical-pull movement) on another day, keeping sets short.
