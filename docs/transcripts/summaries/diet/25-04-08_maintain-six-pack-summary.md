# How to maintain a six-pack without hard work
2025-04-08

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7MFRfujvzGU) Woods opens with a blunt claim: "If you're working hard for your six pack, you're not keeping it."

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript. This extends his other nutrition/leanness content specifically to the problem of *maintaining* a lean body composition long-term rather than just reaching it once.

## Two stated pitfalls that prevent lasting leanness
1. **A gap in the diet** (insufficient protein) — see the protein-target and appetite-regulation mechanism described in his other nutrition content (protein target: 1.8 g/kg goal bodyweight/day, typically hit via one large daily meal).
2. **Being "too small"** — insufficient muscle mass to sustainably support a lean body composition at a comfortable body weight.

## The "healthy weight range" model
- Describes each person's body as having a weight range it will comfortably maintain, largely governed by current muscle mass — describes this range using a rubber-band analogy: staying within it is comfortable and stable; pushing body weight below the bottom of that range (attempting to get leaner than current muscle mass comfortably supports) creates increasing physiological pushback (hunger, rebound weight regain) the further below the threshold a person goes.
- Personal account: attributes his own historical experience of cutting down, getting temporarily lean, then rebounding, to repeatedly dropping below this threshold rather than to any inherent inability to "stay lean" — reframes the rebound pattern as a predictable response to going too far, not evidence that lasting leanness is impossible for him.

## Three-step process for lasting leanness
### Step 1 — Get "lean enough" (once, at current muscle mass)
- Diet and strength training already in place (see other summarized nutrition/training content); run a calorie deficit only until it starts to become genuinely difficult (increased hunger, reduced enjoyment) — stop there rather than pushing further, since that difficulty is the signal of approaching the bottom of the healthy weight range.
- Notes this initial "lean enough" point may not yet be a visible six-pack if starting with limited muscle mass — describes it as an athletic, healthy-looking baseline to build from, not necessarily peak definition.

### Step 2 — Build muscle at this new baseline
- Stop the calorie deficit, resume eating to appetite while continuing strength training focused on progressive overload.
- Claims that because this phase starts from within (not below) the healthy weight range, the body has no urgency to store fat — commonly reports clients getting visibly leaner during this phase even as body weight increases, because muscle gain outpaces any fat gain (or fat is lost concurrently even in a slight surplus).

### Step 3 — Repeat the cycle, ratcheting toward greater leanness over time
- As muscle mass increases, the healthy weight range itself shifts upward, meaning a leaner body composition becomes achievable at the new (higher) body weight without dropping below the threshold — allows a second, easier round of Step 1 (deficit) to reach a new, leaner "lean enough" point than before.
- Suggested cadence: roughly 3 months of muscle-building (eating to appetite, training) per 1 month of deficit as a starting ratio, i.e. up to ~12 weeks/year in deficit — matching the annual structure described in his other summarized nutrition content (e.g. "why trying to stay lean is killing your muscle gains").
- States that over successive years, the proportion of time spent in deficit tends to decrease as the achievable "default" leanness improves purely from accumulated muscle mass.

## Overall framing
- Positions occasional fat gain during a muscle-building phase as an expected, non-threatening part of the process (not a failure) — since the deficit-phase skill is already established and can always be reapplied.
- Attributes his own current low-effort, year-round leanness to roughly a decade of accumulated muscle-building via this repeated cycle, rather than to any special genetics or ongoing restrictive effort.
