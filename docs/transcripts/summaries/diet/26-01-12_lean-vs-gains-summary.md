# Why trying to stay lean is killing your muscle gains
2026-01-12

[In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WFyLIoTYqDQ) Woods identifies the trap: for people who are already lean, "the thing you're most proud of is exactly what's holding you back."

Below is a paraphrased summary, not a transcript.

## Core argument
- Aimed specifically at people who are already lean, training hard, and still not gaining muscle — the diagnosis given is insufficient energy intake (not training quality), caused by prioritizing staying lean over eating enough to fuel growth.
- Three stated reasons a dedicated muscle-gain (calorie surplus) phase is necessary for already-lean people:
  1. **Muscle enables lower sustainable leanness** — being lean with too little muscle reads as "skinny," not defined; the achievable/maintainable body-fat floor is tied to total muscle mass, so further improvement past a point requires more muscle, not more fat loss.
  2. **Muscle growth costs energy, and this cost increases with training experience/leanness** — building muscle in a deficit is described as viable early on but decreasingly viable as a lean, experienced trainee — describes personally plateauing for years while training hard but under-eating, mistakenly treating it as a training problem.
  3. **Committing to a surplus phase means accepting not being at peak leanness for a while** — framed primarily as a psychological hurdle (fear of "losing progress") rather than a technical one.

## Personal example given
- Went from ~70 kg to ~80 kg over several years at a similar body-fat level (i.e., substantial muscle gain) once committing to sustained surplus phases; reports this broke a long strength plateau and led to reaching long-standing strength goals relatively quickly once adequately fed.

## Protocol for the surplus phase
- **Sizing the surplus:** roughly 100–300 kcal/day above maintenance is stated as the useful range for maximizing the muscle-gain rate; more than that is framed as not producing additional muscle-gain benefit.
- **Simplified execution:** get a nutritious dietary base in place (his stated method: one well-planned high-protein meal per day, consistent with his other nutrition content), then let appetite guide additional intake — if hungry, eat more, rather than strict tracking. Tracking calories and a firm daily target is offered as an alternative for those wanting more precision, especially early in the process.
- **Monitoring:** track body weight daily to watch the trend rather than day-to-day noise; expect an initial 1–2 kg jump when transitioning out of a deficit, then a steady increase of roughly 0.5–1 kg/month as the target ongoing rate.
- **Necessary condition, not sufficient:** emphasizes that the calorie surplus only works if training also continues progressing (i.e. progressive overload, ideally with measurable strength increases at least monthly) — without genuine training progress, the extra calories don't get converted into muscle, so a stalled surplus phase should prompt increasing intake by roughly 100–200 kcal/day rather than assuming diet alone will drive gains.

## Structuring surplus and cutting phases across a year
- Recommends the calorie surplus period run a minimum of ~3 months, with 3–9 months given as the optimal range (longer periods favored as training experience increases).
- Rule of thumb for cutting: once already relatively lean, cap deficit/fat-loss periods at ~12 weeks per year total — leaving roughly 9 months/year for a sustained gaining phase.
- Presents two example annual structures: several shorter surplus blocks (e.g. 12 weeks) each followed by a brief (~4-week) cut, or one long seasonal surplus phase (up to ~9 months) followed by a single longer (~12-week) cut — choice framed as personal preference, with motivation/enjoyment used as the practical cue for when to switch phases within these limits.
