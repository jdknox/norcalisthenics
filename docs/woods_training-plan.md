# Woods Training Method — reference

Source: Jack H. Woods (RINGSTRONG), primarily the transcript of
*"The most effective training routine"* (jackhwoods.com, 2026-04-11),
with set/rest figures cross-checked against *"Full week of training"*,
*"How to hack fitness…"*, and *"How to get jacked with bodyweight training"*.

Everything below is his stated position, not an interpretation, unless
marked otherwise.

---

## The six movement patterns

1. Horizontal push
2. Horizontal pull
3. Vertical push
4. Vertical pull
5. Squat
6. Knee curl (reverse squat / hamstring)

One movement per pattern, trained once per week, with the sole intent of
getting as strong as possible at it.

---

## Default weekly split

His own routine — used 90% of the time, and what he recommends to all
students who are free to structure their week however they like.

| Day | Session | Movements |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Horizontal | horizontal push + horizontal pull |
| Tuesday (or Wednesday) | Legs | squat + knee curl |
| Thursday **or** Friday | Vertical | vertical push + vertical pull |

- **Monday is the anchor of the week.** The paired movements are
  antagonists — literally opposite directions — so the first has
  essentially no negative impact on the second, provided the rest between
  them is good.
- **Vertical session sits three to four days after Monday.** Thursday
  gives 3 days then 4; Friday gives 4 days then 3. He says it doesn't
  matter which, and varies it week to week depending on who's around to
  train with and what he feels like.
- **Legs can go anywhere.** Usually Tuesday just to get it done, sometimes
  Wednesday. Placement doesn't matter as long as it's on its own day, so
  the systemic fatigue dissipates.
- Specific days of the week are arbitrary; he just likes starting on
  Monday.

### The pairing rule

**Antagonists together. Same-direction movements three to four days apart.**

- Horizontal push must not share a session with vertical push — both hit
  triceps, shoulders, chest.
- Horizontal pull must not share a session with vertical pull — both hit
  biceps, upper back, rear delts, lats.

Violating this means the second movement is trained on gassed muscles, so
you can't push near maximum intensity and the growth signal is weakened.

### Physiological basis he gives

- ~48 hours of elevated muscle protein synthesis after training a muscle
  group.
- ~72 hours for most of the fatigue from that session to dissipate.
- Hence: about three days before training the same muscles again.

---

## Session structure

- Two movements per session is the sweet spot. Three is still possible.
  Four or more and each successive movement gets much harder to push on,
  overall intensity drops from the start, and motivation/adherence suffers.
- **Rest between the paired movements: at least five minutes.** No upper
  bound — elsewhere he says you could rest an entire day between the two
  if you wanted; he pairs them purely for convenience.
- His arithmetic for a session: 2–3 min first movement, 5 min rest,
  2–3 min second movement → **~10 minutes total including rest**.
- **Start each session with the movement you most want to push progress
  on**, so you can give it everything.

### Where "40 minutes a week" comes from

Six movements back to back in a single session with five minutes rest
between each ≈ 40 minutes. That is the **worst case** — the full-body
single-session version he calls the most time-consuming, difficult method.

- Split into three paired sessions: ~30 minutes total elapsed.
- Actual working time: **12–18 minutes per week**.

---

## Set and rep parameters

- **One set per movement per week**, taken to failure.
- **1–3 minutes per set.** Rep count is irrelevant; time under maximum
  effort is the variable.
- Within the set, scale difficulty down as you fatigue to keep moving,
  and scale back up as soon as it gets easy — the goal is to stay stuck.
- **Stop when it's no longer fun / you feel you've done enough.** His
  stated stopping criterion, explicitly subjective.

---

## Progression

- Test on the **first, freshest rep of the set**.
- If you can get **any partial ROM** at the next-harder level, you're
  cleared to start using it. A full clean rep at a level means it's
  already too easy.
- If you can't get any ROM at the harder version, hold your current level.
- The gate is **finding a sticking point** — a position where you
  physically can't go further and have to grind against it. No sticking
  point means the level is too easy.

Progression is an if/then gate tested every session, not a calendar scheme.

---

## Alternative routines he endorses

**Two sessions per week** (what he uses while travelling): move squats onto
the Monday upper-body session and knee curls onto the vertical day. Each
becomes a full-body session. Longer, harder, expect to be wrecked for half
an hour to an hour afterward.

**One session per week:** all six movements, ~40 min. Works, but the second
half of the week makes less progress and the later movements suffer.

**Six sessions, one movement per day:** completely valid. He declines it
only because training every day isn't worth the marginal benefit — not
because the training is worse.

**Adherence beats optimisation.** His explicit closing point: whatever
routine gets all six patterns trained once a week consistently is the right
one.

## Notes for my setup

- Legs once a week is defended specifically on fatigue grounds — squats are
  extremely taxing, and a full week of recovery produces higher-quality
  training. He does 1–2 reps a week and would only add a second leg day for
  someone already making consistent gains on the basic setup. Relevant
  given the right-knee meniscectomy history.
- My own alternative ordering (vertical Mon / legs Wed / horizontal Fri)
  satisfies the same pairing rule and is equally valid.
